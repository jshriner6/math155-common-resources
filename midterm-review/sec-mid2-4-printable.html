    2.

    Consider the function \(g(t)=t^{2}+3.\)
    1. Find the average rate of change in \(g(t)\) between time \(t=2\) and \(t=2.5.\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Find the average rate of change of \(g(t)\) between time \(t=2\) and time \(t=2+h\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Find the instantaneous rate of change of \(g(t)\) at \(t=2\) using your answer from part (b) and the limit definition of the derivative.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. \(\displaystyle \frac{g(2.5)- g(2)}{2.5-2} = 4.5\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle \frac{g(2+h)-g(2)}{h} = \frac{4h+h^2}{h}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\displaystyle g'(2) = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{g(2+h)-g(2)}{h} =\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{4h+h^2}{h} = 4 \)
      🔗
      🔗

    5.

    1. Find the linear approximation of \(e^{x}\) centered at \(a = 1\text{.}\) Denote this \(L(x)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Find the quadratic approximation of \(e^{x}\) centered at \(a = 1\text{.}\) Denote this \(Q(x)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Evaluate \(L(1.1)\) and \(Q(1.1)\text{.}\) These two values give approximations of what number?
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Do you expect \(L(1.1)\) to be an overestimate or an underestimate of the value it is approximating? Sketch a graph to justify your answer.
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Do you expect \(L(1.1)\) or \(Q(1.1)\) to be a better approximation?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. \(\displaystyle L(x) = e + e(x-1)\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle Q(x) = e + e(x_1) + \frac{e}{2}(x-1)^2\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(e^{1.1} \approx L(1.1) = e +e(0.1) \approx 2.990\)
      🔗
      \(e^{1.1} \approx Q(1.1) = e +e(0.1) +\frac{e}{2}(0.1)^2 \approx 3.004\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. We expect an underestimate, as seen on the graph of \(e^x\) with its linear approximation at \(x=1\).
      🔗
      🔗
    5. \(Q(1.1)\text{,}\) since \(Q(x)\) also matches \(e^x\) in concavity at \(x=1\text{.}\)
      🔗

    You know that a function \(k(x)\) satisfies the following criteria:
    • \(\displaystyle k(2) =3\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle k'(2) = -1\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(k''(x) < 0\) for all \(x\)
      🔗
      🔗
    1. You have enough information to write the linear approximation \(L(x)\) for \(k(x)\) centered at which \(x\)-value? Write the equation of \(L(x)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Is it more appropriate to use \(L(x)\) to estimate \(k(-2.25)\) or \(k(2.25)\text{?}\) Why?
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Use \(L(x)\) to estimate the value that you chose above. Is your estimate an overestimate or underestimate of the true value?
      🔗
      🔗

    Solution.
    1. The center is \(x=2\text{,}\) and \(L(x) = 3 - (x-2) = -x+5\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(k(2.25)\text{,}\) since \(x=2.25\) is closest to the center.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(k(2.25) \approx L(2.25) = 2.75\text{.}\) This is an overestimate, since \(k\) is concave down.

    8.

    Consider the modified logistic dynamical system \(z_{t+1}=rz_{t}(3-z_{t})\text{.}\)
    1. Determine all of the positive equilibrium points of this system.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. For each positive equilibrium point, determine the values of \(r\) which make that point stable using the Stability Theorem.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. Solve \(z^*=rz^*(3-z^*)\) to find \(z^*= \frac{3r-1}{r}\text{,}\) which is positive when \(r \gt \frac{1}{3}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(f(x) = rx(3-x) = 3rx-rx^2\text{,}\) so \(f'(x) = 3r-2rx\text{.}\)
      🔗
      \(f'\left(\frac{3r-1}{r}\right) = 2-3r \text{,}\) so \(z^*\) is stable when \(-1 \lt 2-3r \lt 1\text{,}\) or when \(\frac{1}{3} \lt r \lt 1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    9.

    Find the absolute maximum and absolute minimum values of \(g(x) = \sqrt{x}-x\) on the domain \([0,2]\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We find critical numbers of \(g\) by solving \(g'(x) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}} - 1 = 0\) and also where \(g'(x)\) does not exist. \(g'(x) = 0\) when \(x=0.25\) and \(g'(x)\) does not exist when \(x=0\text{,}\) which are both in the domain we are considering.
    🔗
    \(g(0) = 0\text{,}\) \(g(0.25)= 0.25\text{,}\) and \(g(2) = \sqrt{2}-2\text{.}\) So the absolute maximum of \(g\) is \(0.25\) (and is achieved at \(x=0.25\)), and the absolute minimum is \(sqrt{2}-2\) (and is achieved at \(x=2\)).
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    10.

    Find the critical number(s) of \(f(x) = 1 + 2x^{2} + x^{\frac{1}{3}}\) and classify them as being local minima, local maxima, or neither of the function \(f(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(f'(x) = 4x+ \frac{1}{3}x^{-2/3}\text{,}\) which is zero at \(x= (-1/12)^{3/5}\text{,}\) and does not exist at \(x=0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Using the first derivative test, we can test three \(x\)-values: one less than \(x= (-1/12)^{3/5}\text{,}\) one between \(x= (-1/12)^{3/5}\) and \(x=0\text{,}\) and one greater than \(x=0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We find that \(f'(-1) \lt 0\) (so \(f\) is decreasing), \(f'(-0.1) \gt 0\) (so \(f\) is increasing), and \(f'(1) \gt 0\) (so \(f\) is increasing). This means \(x= (-1/12)^{3/5}\) is the location of a local minimum of \(f\text{,}\) and \(x=0\) is neither.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

      🔗
      🔗
    3. What does the second derivative test say about each critical number?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. \(\displaystyle x=-1,3\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(x=-1\) is a local minimum, while \(x=3\) is neither. A first derivative sign chart should change from negative to positive at \(x=-1\) (so the behavior of \(f\) is decreasing to increasing), and from positive to positive at \(x=3\) (so the behavior of \(f\) is increasing to increasing).
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(h''(-1) \gt 0\text{,}\) so the second derivative test says that \(x=-1\) is a local minimum.
      🔗
      \(h''(3) = 0\text{,}\) so the second derivative test is inconclusive.
